L6@P0: Programmers and researchers are increasingly developing surrogates of programs, models of a subset of the
L7@P0: observable behavior of a given program, to solve a variety of software development challenges. Programmers
L8@P0: train surrogates from measurements of the behavior of a program on a dataset of input examples. A key challenge
L9@P0: of surrogate construction is determining what training data to use to train a surrogate of a given program.
L10@P0: We present a methodology for sampling datasets to train neural-network-based surrogates of programs. We
L11@P0: first characterize the proportion of data to sample from each region of a program’s input space (corresponding to
L12@P0: different execution paths of the program) based on the complexity of learning a surrogate of the corresponding
L13@P0: execution path. We next provide a program analysis to determine the complexity of different paths in a program.
L14@P0: We evaluate these results on a range of real-world programs, demonstrating that complexity-guided sampling
L15@P0: results in empirical improvements in accuracy.

L22@P0: 1 INTRODUCTION
L23@P0: Surrogate programming – the act of replacing a program with a surrogate model of its behavior –
L24@P0: has been increasingly leveraged to solve a variety of software development challenges [Renda et al.
L25@P0: 2021]. For example, Esmaeilzadeh et al. [2012] train neural networks to mimic existing numerical
L26@P0: programs (a fast Fourier transform, a triangle intersection detection algorithm, a JPEG encoder, etc.)
L27@P0: and execute these neural networks on specialized hardware, achieving an average speedup of 2.3×
L28@P0: across benchmarks at the cost of 10% accuracy in these numerical programs. Kustowski et al. [2020]
L29@P0: train a neural network to mimic computer simulations of inertial confinement fusion, then fine-tune
L30@P0: the neural network on a small amount of data from real experiments to improve the accuracy of the
L31@P0: simulated predictions. Renda et al. [2020] train a neural network to mimic a CPU simulator, then
L32@P0: exploit the fact that this neural surrogate is differentiable to optimize the CPU simulator’s simulation
L33@P0: parameters with gradient descent.

L45@P1: 1.1 Surrogate Programming
L46@P1: Beyond these examples, surrogate programming has developed into a diverse set of techniques and
L47@P1: applications across many areas of computing and science. Renda et al. [2021] organize the uses of
L48@P1: neural network based surrogates of programs into three categories.
L49@P1: Surrogate compilation. In surrogate compilation, programmers develop a surrogate that replicates
L50@P1: the behavior of a program to deploy to end-users in place of that program. For example, in addition to
L51@P1: Esmaeilzadeh et al.[2012] (who use a surrogate to speed up numerical programs), Mendis[2020] use a
L52@P1: surrogate to speed up compiler autovectorization by replacing an integer linear program (ILP) solver
L53@P1: with a surrogate. Key benefits of surrogate compilation include the ability to execute the surrogate
L54@P1: on different hardware and the ability to bound or to accelerate the execution time of the surrogate.
L55@P1: Surrogate adaptation. In surrogate adaptation, programmers first develop a surrogate of a program
L56@P1: and then further train that surrogate on data from a different task. For example, in addition to
L57@P1: Kustowski et al. [2020] (who use a surrogate to improve the accuracy of inertial confinement fusion
L58@P1: simulations), Tercan et al. [2018] use this technique to improve the accuracy of computer simulations
L59@P1: of plastic injection molding. Key benefits of surrogate adaptation include that it makes it possible
L60@P1: to alter the semantics of the program to perform a different task of interest and that it may be more
L61@P1: data-efficient or result in higher accuracy than training a model from scratch for the task.
L62@P1: Surrogate optimization. In surrogate optimization programmers develop a surrogate of a program,
L63@P1: use gradient descent against the surrogate to identify program inputs that optimize a downstream
L64@P1: objective, then use the inputs for executing the original program. In addition to Renda et al. [2020]
L65@P1: (who use this technique to optimize CPU simulation parameters),Tseng et al.[2019] use this technique
L66@P1: to find parameters for camera pipelines that lead to the most photorealistic images. She et al. [2019]
L67@P1: use this technique to find inputs that trigger branches that may cause bugs in the original program.
L68@P1: The key benefit of surrogate optimization is that it can identify optimal inputs faster than optimizing
L69@P1: directly against the program, due to the faster execution speed of the surrogate and that the surrogate
L70@P1: is differentiable even when the original program is not (allowing the use of gradient descent).
L71@P1: 1.2 Dataset Generation
L72@P1: In each of these scenarios, training a surrogate of a program requires measuring the behavior of
L73@P1: the program on a dataset of input examples. There are three common approaches to collecting this
L74@P1: dataset. The first is to use data instrumented from running the original program on a workload of
L75@P1: interest [Esmaeilzadeh et al. 2012; Renda et al. 2020]. In the absence of an available workload, another
L76@P1: is to uniformly sample(or sample using another manually defined distribution) from the input space of
L77@P1: the program [Kustowski et al. 2020; Tseng et al. 2019]. The third is to use active learning [Settles 2009],
L78@P1: a class of online methods that iteratively query labels for the data points that are most useful (however
L79@P1: defined) for further training the surrogate [İpek et al. 2006; Pestourie et al. 2020; She et al. 2019].
L80@P1: Each of these approaches face challenges on programs with different behaviors in different regions
L81@P1: of the input space. For example, Renda et al. [2020, Section IV.A] identify a scenario in which an
L82@P1: instrumented dataset does not exercise a set of control flow paths in the program enough times for the
L83@P1: surrogate to learn the program’s behavior along those paths, resulting in a surrogate that generates
L84@P1: highly inaccurate predictions for inputs in the regions of the input space corresponding to those paths.
L85@P1: 1.3 Our Approach: Complexity-Guided Sampling
L86@P1: Rather than treating the program as a black box, our approach uses the source code and semantics
L87@P1: of the program under study to guide dataset generation for training a surrogate of the program.
L88@P1: The core concept is to allocate samples based on both the the complexity of learning the program’s
L89@P1: behavior on a given path and the frequency of that path in the input data distribution.
L90@P1-2: Proc. ACM Program. Lang., Vol. 7, No. OOPSLA2, Article 280. Publication date: October 2023.Turaco: Complexity-Guided Data Sampling for Training Neural Surrogates of Programs 280:3
L91@P2: Complexity-guided sampling. Our objective is to find how many samples to allocate to each region
L92@P2: of the input space to minimize the expected error of the resulting surrogate. To reason about the error
L93@P2: of a surrogate, we use neural network sample complexity bounds for learning analytic functions [Agarwala et al. 2021; Arora et al. 2019]. These bounds give an upper bound on how many samples are
L94@P2: required to learn a surrogate of an analytic function to a given error as a function of a complexity
L95@P2: measure of that function. Our approach calculates a complexity measure for the function induced by
L96@P2: each control flow path in the program and combines that with the frequency of each path according
L97@P2: to an input data distribution. The output of the approach is the proportion of samples to allocate
L98@P2: to each region of the input space, minimizing an upper bound on the stratified surrogate’s error.
L99@P2: Stratified functions. Our core modeling assumption is to represent the program as a stratified
L100@P2: function, a piecewise1function across different regions (strata) of the input space. We use stratified
L101@P2: surrogates to model such functions. To construct a stratified surrogate, we train independent surrogates of each component of the stratified function. During evaluation, a stratified surrogate uses the
L102@P2: original program to check which stratum an input is in, then applies the corresponding surrogate.
L103@P2: Complexity analysis. We present a programming language, Turaco, in which programs denote
L104@P2: stratified functions with well-defined complexity measures (specifically, stratified analytic functions).
L105@P2: We provide a static program analysis for Turaco programs that automatically calculates an upper
L106@P2: bound on the complexity of each component of the stratified function that the program denotes.
L107@P2: Evaluation. To demonstrate that complexity-guided sampling using our complexity analysis
L108@P2: improves surrogate error on downstream tasks, we evaluate our approach on a range of programs,
L109@P2: finding that across this selection of programs complexity-guided sampling improves error relative to
L110@P2: baseline distributions by around 5%.We demonstrate that a 5%improvementin error of a surrogate can
L111@P2: result in a 28%improvement in execution speed in an application with a maximum error threshold.We
L112@P2: then analyze the classes of problems for which complexity-guided sampling excels, finding potential
L113@P2: improvementsin error of up to 30%, and the classes of problems for which complexity-guided sampling
L114@P2: using Turaco’s complexity analysis sampling fails, finding deteriorations in error of up to 500%.
L115@P2: Renderer demonstration. We further present a case study of learning a surrogate of a renderer
L116@P2: in a video game engine. We show that our complexity-guided sampling approach results in between 17% and 44% lower error than training using baseline distributions that do not take into
L117@P2: account path complexity. These error improvements correspond with perceptual improvements
L118@P2: in the generated renders.
L119@P2: Contributions. We present the following contributions:
L120@P2: • An approach to allocating samples among strata to train stratified neural network surrogates
L121@P2: of stratified analytic functions that minimizes an upper bound on the surrogate’s error.
L122@P2: • A programming language, Turaco, in which all programs are stratified analytic functions, and
L123@P2: a program analysis to bound the complexity of learning surrogates of those programs.
L124@P2: • Empirical evaluations on real-world programs demonstrating that complexity-guided sampling
L125@P2: using Turaco’s complexity analysis results in empirical improvements in error, and that these
L126@P2: improvements in error result in improvements in downstream applications.
L127@P2: • Further empirical evaluations of the classes of problems where complexity-guided sampling
L128@P2: using Turaco’s complexity analysis succeeds and fails.
L129@P2: We lay the groundwork for analyzing complexity-guided sampling approaches for training surrogates
L130@P2: of programs. Our results hold out the promise of surrogate training approaches that intelligently
L131@P2: use the program’s semantics to guide the design and training of surrogates of programs.

L236@P4: Complexity-guided sampling. Our objective is to find the number of data points to sample from each
L237@P4: path to minimize the expectation of error of a surrogate of the overall program, given a data distribution and a data budget. To accomplish this, our approach leverages the complexity of each path and
L238@P4: the frequency of each path in the data distribution, prioritizing sampling paths that are more complex
L239@P4: (requiring more samples to learn) and that are more frequent (and thus more important to learn).
L240@P4: First our approach determines the sample complexity of each trace along each path, the number
L241@P4: of samples required to learn a surrogate of the trace (in isolation) to a given error. Our approach
L242@P4: extends the sample complexity results of Agarwala et al. [2021], who give an upper bound on the
L243@P4: number of samples required to learn a neural network approximation of a given analytic function.
L244@P4: Using this bound (as implemented by our Turaco analysis described in Section 4), our approach
L245@P4: determines that the twilight path takes 1.4× as many samples to train a surrogate to a given error
L246@P4: as the nighttime path, and the daytime path requires 3.7× as many samples.
L247@P4: Then given a distribution with the frequency of each path, our approach determines the complexityguided sampling rates for each path. In this example we assume that the data has a uniform
L248@P4: distribution over inputs between −1 and 1, resulting in path frequencies for the nighttime path
L249@P4: (sunPosition < 0) of 50%, the twilight path (0 < sunPosition < 0.1) of 10%, and the daytime path
L250@P4: (0.1 < sunPosition) of 40%. With this, our approach determines that the nighttime path should
L251@P4: be sampled at 36.9% of the data budget (undersampling relative to its frequency because it is simple
L252@P4: to learn), the twilight path at 14.0%, and the daytime path at 49.1% (oversampling relative to its
L253@P4: frequency because it is complex to learn).
L254@P4: Stratified surrogates. The class of surrogate model for which we derive the above approach is that
L255@P4: of a stratified neural surrogate – a set of disjoint neural networks which are applied based on which
L256@P4: path the inputs induce in the program. Concretely, this means that we train one surrogate per path,
L257@P4: and pick which to apply for each input at evaluation time. For this example program, picking which
L258@P4: surrogate to apply just requires comparing sunPosition against constant threshold values.
