L13@P0: 1. Introduction. One of the basic primitives in the study of the interaction
L14@P0: between randomness and feasible computation is a pseudorandom generator. Intuitively, a pseudorandom generator is a polynomial time-computable function g that
L15@P0: stretches a short random string x into a long string g(x) that “looks” random to any
L16@P0: feasible algorithm, called an adversary. The adversary tries to distinguish the string
L17@P0: g(x) from a random string the same length as g(x). The two strings “look” the same
L18@P0: to the adversary if the acceptance probability for both strings is essentially the same.
L19@P0: Thus, a pseudorandom generator can be used to efficiently convert a small amount of
L20@P0: true randomness into a much larger number of effectively random bits.
L21@P0: The notion of randomness tests for a string evolved over time: from set-theoretic
L22@P0: tests to enumerable [K65], recursive, and finally limited time tests. Motivated by cryptographic applications, the seminal paper [BM82] introduced the idea of a generator
L23@P0: which produces its output in polynomial time such that its output passes a general
L24@P0: polynomial time test. The fundamental paper [Yao82] introduced the definition of a
L25@P0: pseudorandom generator most commonly used today and proves that this definition
L26@P0: and the original of [BM82] are equivalent.
L27@P0: The robust notion of a pseudorandom generator, due to [BM82], [Yao82], should
L28@P0: be contrasted with the classical methods of generating random looking bits as described in, e.g., [Knuth97]. In studies of classical methods, the output of the generator
L29@P0: is considered good if it passes a particular set of standard statistical tests. The linear congruential generator is an example of a classical method for generating random
L30@P0: looking bits that pass a variety of standard statistical tests. However, [Boyar89] and
L31@P0: [K92] show that there is a polynomial time statistical test which the output from this

L52@P1: In the context of Monte Carlo simulation applications, a typical algorithm uses
L53@P1: long random strings, and a typical analysis shows that the algorithm produces a correct answer with high probability if the string it uses is chosen uniformly. In practice,
L54@P1: the long random string is not chosen uniformly, as this would require more random
L55@P1: bits than it is typically reasonable to produce (and store). Instead, a short random

L108@P2: 1.1. Concepts and tools. Previous methods, following [BM82], rely on constructing a function that has an output bit that is computationally unpredictable
L109@P2: given the other bits of the output, but is nevertheless statistically correlated with
L110@P2: these other bits. [GL89] provide a simple and natural input bit which is hidden from
L111@P2: (a padded version of) any one-way function. Their result radically simplifies the previous constructions of pseudorandom generators from one-way permutations and in
L112@P2: addition makes all previous constructions substantially more efficient. We use their
L113@P2: result in a fundamental way.
L114@P2: Our overall approach is different in spirit from previous constructions of pseudorandom generators based on one-way functions with special structure. Previous
L115@P2: methods rely on iterating the one-way function many times, and from each iteration
L116@P2: they extract a computationally unpredictable bit. The approach is to make sure that
L117@P2: after many iterations the function is still one-way. In contrast, as explained below in
L118@P2: more detail, our approach concentrates on extracting and smoothing entropy in parallel from many independent copies of the one-way function. Our overall construction
L119@P2: combines this parallel approach with a standard method for iteratively stretching the
L120@P2: output of a pseudorandom generator.
L121@P2: The notion of computational indistinguishability provides one of the main conceptual tools in our paper. Following [GM84] and [Yao82], we say that two probability
L122@P2: distributions D and E are computationally indistinguishable if no feasible adversary
L123@P2: can distinguish D from E. In these terms, a pseudorandom generator is intuitively the
L124@P2: following: let g be a polynomial time computable function that maps strings of length
L125@P2: n to longer strings of length ℓn > n. Let X be a random variable that is uniformly
L126@P2: distributed on strings of length n and let Y be a random variable that is uniformly
L127@P2: distributed on strings of length ℓn. Then g is a pseudorandom generator if g(X) and
L128@P2: Y are computationally indistinguishable.
