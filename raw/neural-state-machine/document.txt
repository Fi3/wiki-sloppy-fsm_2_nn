L9@P0: Abstract
L10@P0: We introduce the Neural State Machine, seeking to bridge the gap between the
L11@P0: neural and symbolic views of AI and integrate their complementary strengths for
L12@P0: the task of visual reasoning. Given an image, we first predict a probabilistic graph
L13@P0: that represents its underlying semantics and serves as a structured world model.
L14@P0: Then, we perform sequential reasoning over the graph, iteratively traversing its
L15@P0: nodes to answer a given question or draw a new inference. In contrast to most
L16@P0: neural architectures that are designed to closely interact with the raw sensory
L17@P0: data, our model operates instead in an abstract latent space, by transforming both
L18@P0: the visual and linguistic modalities into semantic concept-based representations,
L19@P0: thereby achieving enhanced transparency and modularity. We evaluate our model
L20@P0: on VQA-CP and GQA, two recent VQA datasets that involve compositionality,
L21@P0: multi-step inference and diverse reasoning skills, achieving state-of-the-art results
L22@P0: in both cases. We provide further experiments that illustrate the model’s strong
L23@P0: generalization capacity across multiple dimensions, including novel compositions
L24@P0: of concepts, changes in the answer distribution, and unseen linguistic structures,
L25@P0: demonstrating the qualities and efficacy of our approach.
L26@P0: 1 Introduction
L27@P0: Language is one of the most marvelous feats of the human mind. The emergence of a compositional
L28@P0: system of symbols that can distill and convey from rich sensory experiences to creative new ideas has
L29@P0: been a major turning point in the evolution of intelligence, and made a profound impact on the nature
L30@P0: of human cognition [19, 78, 13]. According to Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought hypothesis [22, 72],
L31@P0: thinking itself posses a language-like compositional structure, where elementary concepts combine in
L32@P0: systematic ways to create compound new ideas or thoughts, allowing us to make “infinite use of finite
L33@P0: means” [18] and fostering human’s remarkable capacities of abstraction and generalization [51].
L34@P0: Indeed, humans are particularly adept at making abstractions of various kinds: We make analogies
L35@P0: and form concepts to generalize from given instances to unseen examples [70]; we see things in
L36@P0: context, and build compositional world models to represent objects and understand their interactions
L37@P0: and subtle relations, turning raw sensory signals into high-level semantic knowledge [64]; and we
L38@P0: deductively draw inferences via conceptual rules and statements to proceed from known facts to novel
L39@P0: conclusions [32, 40]. Not only are humans capable of learning, but we are also talented at reasoning.
L40@P0: Ideas about compositionality, abstraction and reasoning greatly inspired the classical views of
L41@P0: artificial intelligence [74, 65], but have lately been overshadowed by the astounding success of deep
L42@P0: learning over a wide spectrum of real-world tasks [33, 63, 82]. Yet, even though neural networks
L43@P0: are undoubtedly powerful, flexible and robust, recent work has repeatedly demonstrated their flaws,
L44@P0: showing how they struggle to generalize in a systematic manner [50], overly adhere to superficial and
L45@P0: potentially misleading statistical associations instead of learning true causal relations [1, 42], strongly
L46@P0: depend on large amounts of data and supervision [25, 51], and sometimes behave in surprising and
L47@P0: 33rd Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2019), Vancouver, Canada.

L53@P1: Motivated to alleviate these deficiencies and bring the neural and symbolic approaches more closely
L54@P1: together, we propose the Neural State Machine, a differentiable graph-based model that simulates
L55@P1: the operation of an automaton, and explore it in the domain of visual reasoning and compositional
L56@P1: question answering. Essentially, we proceed through two stages: modeling and inference. Starting
L57@P1: from an image, we first generate a probabilistic scene graph [43, 49] that captures its underlying
L58@P1: semantic knowledge in a compact form. Nodes correspond to objects and consist of structured
L59@P1: representations of their properties, and edges depict both their spatial and semantic relations. Once
L60@P1: we have the graph, we then treat it as a state machine and simulate an iterative computation over
L61@P1: it, aiming to answer questions or draw inferences. We translate a given natural language question
L62@P1: into a series of soft instructions, and feed them one-at-a-time into the machine to perform sequential
L63@P1: reasoning, using attention to traverse its states and compute the answer.
L64@P1: Drawing inspiration from Bengio’s consciousness prior [12], we further define a set of semantic
L65@P1: embedded concepts that describe different entities and aspects of the domain, such as various kinds
L66@P1: of objects, attributes and relations. These concepts are used as the vocabulary that underlies both the
L67@P1: scene graphs derived from the image as well as the reasoning instructions obtained from the question,
L68@P1: effectively allowing both modalities to “speak the same language”. Whereas neural networks typically
L69@P1: interact directly with raw observations and dense features, our approach encourages the model to
L70@P1: reason instead in a semantic and factorized abstract space, which enables the disentanglement of
L71@P1: structure from content and improves its modularity.
L72@P1: We demonstrate the value and performance of the Neural State Machine on two recent Visual Question
L73@P1: Answering (VQA) datasets: GQA [41] which focuses on real-world visual reasoning and multi-step
L74@P1: question answering, as well as VQA-CP [3], a recent split of the popular VQA dataset [2, 27] that
L75@P1: has been designed particularly to evaluate generalization. We achieve state-of-the-art results on
L76@P1: both tasks under single-model settings, substantiating the robustness and efficiency of our approach
L77@P1: in answering challenging compositional questions. We then construct new splits leveraging the
L78@P1: associated structured representations provided by GQA and conduct further experiments that provide
L79@P1: significant evidence for the model’s strong generalization skills across multiple dimensions, such
L80@P1: as novel compositions of concepts and unseen linguistic structures, validating its versatility under
L81@P1: changing conditions.
L82@P1: Our model ties together two important qualities: abstraction and compositionality, with the respective
L83@P1: key innovations of representing meaning as a structured attention distribution over an internal vocabulary of disentangled concepts, and capturing sequential reasoning as the iterative computation
L84@P1: of a differentiable state machine over a semantic graph. We hope that creating such neural form
L85@P1: of a classical model of computation will encourage and support the integration of the connectionist and symbolic methodologies in AI, opening the door to enhanced modularity, versatility, and
L86@P1: generalization.

L89@P2: 2 Related work
L90@P2: Our model connects to multiple lines of research, including works about compositionality [14, 38],
L91@P2: concept acquisition [36, 81], and neural computation [28, 62, 7]. Several works have explored the
L92@P2: discovery and use of visual concepts in the contexts of reinforcement or unsupervised learning
L93@P2: [35, 17] as well as in classical computer vision [21, 77]. Others have argued for the importance
L94@P2: of incorporating strong inductive biases into neural architectures [14, 10, 6, 8], and indeed, there
L95@P2: is a growing body of research that seeks to introduce different forms of structural priors inspired
L96@P2: by computer architectures [29, 80, 40] or theory of computation [4, 23], aiming to bridge the gap
L97@P2: between the symbolic and neural paradigms.
L98@P2: We explore our model in the context of VQA, a challenging multimodal task that has gained substantial
L99@P2: attention in recent years [27, 79, 40]. Prior work commonly relied on dense visual features produced
L100@P2: by either CNNs [83, 86] or object detectors [5], with a few recent models that use the relationships
L101@P2: among objects to augment those features with contextual information from each object’s surroundings
L102@P2: [52, 75, 66]. We move further in this direction, performing iterative reasoning over inferred scene
L103@P2: graphs, and in contrast to prior models, incorporate higher-level semantic concepts to represent both
L104@P2: the visual and linguistic modalities in a shared and sparser manner that facilitates their interaction.
L105@P2: Closest to the NSM is a model called MAC [40] we have developed in prior work, a recurrent network
L106@P2: that applies attention-based operations to perform sequential reasoning. In fact, our new model
L107@P2: follows the high-level structure proposed by MAC, where the new decoder (section 3.3) is analogous
L108@P2: to the control unit, and the model simulation (section 3.4) is parallel to the read-write operation.

L111@P2: graph by successively shifting attention across its nodes and edges. Second, key to our model is the
