L305@P6: 3. The Conjecture and Attempts to Prove It
L306@P6: Most complexity theorists believe that P 6= NP. Perhaps this can be partly
L307@P6: explained by the potentially stunning consequences of P = NP mentioned above,
L308@P6: but there are better reasons. We explain these by considering the two possibilities
L309@P6: in turn: P = NP and P 6= NP.
L310@P6: Suppose first that P = NP and consider how one might prove it. The obvious way is to exhibit a polynomial-time algorithm for 3-SAT or one of the other
L311@P6: thousand or so known NP-complete problems, and, indeed, many false proofs have
L312@P6: been presented in this form. There is a standard toolkit available [7] for devising
L313@P6: polynomial-time algorithms, including the greedy method, dynamic programming,
L314@P6: reduction to linear programming, etc. These are the subjects of a course on algorithms, typical in undergraduate computer science curriculums. Because of their
L315@P6: importance in industry, a vast number of programmers and engineers have attempted to find efficient algorithms for NP-complete problems over the past 30
L316@P6: years, without success. There is a similar strong motivation for breaking the cryptographic schemes that assume P 6= NP for their security.
L317@P6: Of course, it is possible that some nonconstructive argument, such as the Robertson–Seymour proofs mentioned earlier in conjunction with the Feasibility Thesis,
L318@P6: could show that P = NP without yielding any feasible algorithm for the standard
L319@P6: NP-complete problems. At present, however, the best proven upper bound on
L320@P6: an algorithm for solving 3-SAT is approximately 1.5
L321@P6: n, where n is the number of
L322@P6: variables in the input formula.

L324@P6: There are two general methods that have been tried: diagonalization with reduction
L325@P6: and Boolean circuit lower bounds.
