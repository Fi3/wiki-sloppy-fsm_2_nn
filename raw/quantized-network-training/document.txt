L13@P0: ABSTRACT
L14@P0: The training of neural networks has been extensively studied from both algorithmic and complexity-theoretic perspectives, yet recent results in this direction almost exclusively concern real-valued networks. In contrast, advances in machine
L15@P0: learning practice highlight the benefits of quantization, where network parameters and data are restricted to finite integer domains, yielding significant improvements in speed and energy efficiency. Motivated by this gap, we initiate a systematic complexity-theoretic study of ReLU Neural Network Training in the full
L16@P0: quantization mode. We establish strong lower bounds by showing that hardness
L17@P0: already arises in the binary setting and under highly restrictive structural assumptions on the architecture, thereby excluding parameterized tractability for natural
L18@P0: measures such as depth and width. On the positive side, we identify nontrivial
L19@P0: fixed-parameter tractable cases when parameterizing by input dimensionality in
L20@P0: combination with width and either output dimensionality or error bound, and further strengthen these results by replacing width with the more general treewidth.
L21@P0: 1 INTRODUCTION
L22@P0: A crucial task tied to the use of neural networks is their training. On a high level, this training task
L23@P0: can be characterized as follows: given a neural network architecture G and a data set D of inputoutput pairs, compute weights and biases of G which minimize the error achieved by the network
L24@P0: on D. While we have powerful heuristics for solving this problem (Sze et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022),
L25@P0: it also exhibits highly interesting behavior on the complexity-theoretical level and has been studied
L26@P0: from this perspective in a series of recent foundational papers (Dey et al., 2020; Abrahamsen et al.,
L27@P0: 2021; Goel et al., 2021; Boob et al., 2022; Froese & Hertrich, 2023; Bertschinger et al., 2023; Brand
L28@P0: et al., 2023). A detailed discussion of the state of the art is deferred to the end of this section;
L29@P0: nevertheless, it will be useful to note that for a crisper complexity analysis one typically considers
L30@P0: the equivalent decision formulation of the problem—i.e., where the input also includes an error
L31@P0: bound ℓ and the algorithm is allowed to output “no” if such an error bound cannot be achieved by
L32@P0: any combination of weights and biases.1
L33@P0: 1Technically, in decision problems one is not required to output the weights and biases for positive instances;
L34@P0: however, every algorithm obtained or mentioned in this article is constructive and capable of doing so. We note
L35@P0: that the optimization task can be reduced to the decision formulation via a trivial search routine on ℓ.

L37@P1: A common feature of all the above-mentioned complexity-theoretical works targeting the above
L38@P1: NEURAL NETWORK TRAINING (NNT) problem is that they assume the numbers occurring in the
L39@P1: network to be reals. This is a natural perspective that matches the classical formalization of neural
L40@P1: networks. However, a series of recent advances have shown that one can significantly improve speed
L41@P1: and energy efficiency by quantizing the neural network, i.e., forcing the numbers to lie in a specified
L42@P1: domain of integers (Kilic et al., 2022). For example, Wang et al. (2025) recently showed that one can
L43@P1: achieve accuracy results comparable to the real-valued setting when quantizing to 4 bits, i.e., with a
L44@P1: domain size of 16; see also the preceding works of Yang et al. (2020) and Lin et al. (2022). Other
L45@P1: works have also considered even stronger degrees of quantization, such as using binary domains (Lin
L46@P1: et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020). In fact, several different methods have been developed
L47@P1: to obtain high-quality quantized neural networks such as fully-quantized training (Zhou et al., 2016),
L48@P1: mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., 2018), post-training quantization (Banner et al., 2019),
L49@P1: and quantization-aware training (Jacob et al., 2018).
L50@P1: Yet, the recent developments outlined above are not at all reflected in our understanding of the
L51@P1: underlying foundational problem: neither the complexity-theoretic lower bounds (Dey et al., 2020;
L52@P1: Abrahamsen et al., 2021; Goel et al., 2021; Froese & Hertrich, 2023; Bertschinger et al., 2023), nor
L53@P1: the algorithms underpinning our upper bounds for solving the training problem (Arora et al., 2018;
L54@P1: Boob et al., 2022; Brand et al., 2023) can be translated into the quantized setting. We note that
L55@P1: this does not seem to be merely the case of a missing “bridge” that would allow one to translate
L56@P1: knowledge from one setting to the other—the training problem in the real-valued setting is ∃Rcomplete (Abrahamsen et al., 2021; Bertschinger et al., 2023) but with quantization it is easily seen
L57@P1: to lie in NP (see Section 2), pointing to a fundamental difference between the two settings. Until
L58@P1: now, we lacked any complexity-theoretic study targeting NNT in the fully quantized setting.
L59@P1: The aim of this article is to fill the aforementioned gap by developing a comprehensive understanding
L60@P1: of QUANTIZED RELU-NNT (see Section 2 for formal details and a discussion of the error bound):

L151@P3: Technical Overview. To obtain our lower bounds, we develop targeted reductions from a variety
L152@P3: of problems, including BOOLEAN SATISFIABILITY, HITTING SET, and SET COVER. While each
L153@P3: of the reductions is distinct, the constructed architectures are often very dense and have simple graph
L154@P3: structures. In other words, our results show that the difficulty of training in the quantized setting does
L155@P3: not stem from the complexity of the architecture, but rather from the presence of high-dimensional
L156@P3: data on the input or output. In fact, the main open question arising from our work is whether the converse is true: can we efficiently solve instances of d-QNNT with possibly complicated architectures,
L157@P3: but constant input and output data dimensionality (i.e., α + ω)?
L158@P3: For our positive results—specifically, Theorems 5 and 6—the main technical difficulty is that the
L159@P3: trained n-node networks could contain hidden neurons with Θ(n) incoming arcs from the preceding layer that have non-zero weights. Indeed, it is not difficult to construct instances with
L160@P3: such solutions—and yet the dynamic programming techniques that form the cornerstone of most
L161@P3: treewidth-based algorithms are incapable of efficiently searching for them. To deal with this issue,
L162@P3: we make a detour and first establish a structural insight that we believe is of independent interest:
L163@P3: every YES-instance of d-QNNT admits at least one solution where the number of activated arcs entering any node is upper-bounded by a function of the parameters. This is formalized in Lemma 1,
L164@P3: and relies on an involved proof that builds on Steinitz’ Lemma.
L165@P3: Full proofs and details deferred to the Full version, which can be found in the supplemental material,
L166@P3: are marked with (⋆).

L243@P4: 3 LOWER BOUNDS FOR d-QNNT
L244@P4: In this section, we show that 2-QNNT remains intractable in highly restrictive settings. First, in
L245@P4: Theorem 1, we establish NP-hardness even if the architecture has no hidden neuron, only one output neuron, and for training without error. Note that Theorem 1 implies NP-hardness even when the
L246@P4: combined parameter width + depth + ℓ + ω is upper-bounded by a constant. Naturally, the corresponding reduction requires the output neurons to have an arbitrarily large degree. One could hence
L247@P4: hope that architectures with constant maximum degree can be trained efficiently. In Theorem 2, we
L248@P4: show that this is not possible by establishing NP-hardness for this setting.
L249@P4: In both the reductions that underlie Theorems 1 and 2 the number of input neurons is large and in
L250@P4: particular not upper-bounded by a function of the parameters. Hence, one could hope that a small or
L251@P4: even constant number of inputs allows for efficient training. We show that this is not the case either.
L252@P4: First, in Theorem 3, we provide W[2]-hardness for α even if there is no hidden layer. Second, in
L253@P4: Theorem 4, we show that 2-QNNT remains NP-hard even if there are only 2 inputs and 1 hidden
L254@P4: layer. Altogether, these results yield the lower bounds depicted in Figure 1.
L255@P4: Theorem 1 (⋆). 2-QNNT is NP-hard even when restricted to instances where ℓ = 0 and architectures with a single output neuron and no hidden neuron.
L256@P4: Proof Sketch. We provide a reduction from the NP-hard EXACT SET COVER problem (Karp, 1972)
L257@P4: where the input consists of a universe U, and a family F of subsets over U. The goal is to find a
